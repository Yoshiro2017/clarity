Clarity

A professional, thoughtfully designed digital space built on principles of clarity, accessibility, and respectful design. Every element — from the comprehensive selection of typefaces to the palette, spacing, and interaction patterns — has been chosen with deliberate intent, balancing aesthetic refinement with functional utility. This is not a commercial platform. There are no advertisements, no tracking scripts, no analytics, and no third-party monitoring beyond font delivery. This space exists to present information, share ideas, and demonstrate thoughtful web design in a manner that respects your attention, your time, and your privacy.

The foundation is built entirely on open standards and open-source technology. The Noto font family provides comprehensive typographic support — spanning from global Latin, Cyrillic, and Greek to historical scripts of the ancient world, regional writing systems, and modern calligraphic traditions — with consistent quality across devices and languages. The codebase is minimal, self-contained, and designed to be understood, modified, and extended by anyone without requiring specialized tools or proprietary systems.

In an era where the internet is increasingly cluttered, slow, and opaque, returning to fundamentals is not a limitation — it is a liberation. Semantic HTML5 provides structure that machines and humans alike can understand. CSS3 delivers styling without excessive overhead. Progressive JavaScript enhances experience without becoming a requirement. This approach ensures the site loads quickly, functions reliably, and remains accessible to every visitor regardless of device capability or network speed.

Typography is not merely decoration here. It is the primary medium through which all information passes. Every typeface included has been selected not just for beauty, but for legibility, cultural appropriateness, and completeness. The Noto family — commissioned by Google and developed collaboratively — was created specifically to address gaps in global font coverage. Its name derives from the Latin word "nota," meaning "written mark or character," and its mission is to ensure that all languages can be represented equally on all devices. Where most sites treat non-Latin scripts as afterthoughts or exotic additions, here they are integrated fully, each with appropriate weights, styles, and directionality.

Consider Arabic: written right-to-left, cursive by nature, with letter forms changing dramatically based on position in a word. It requires different visual rhythm, spacing logic, and font choices than Latin text. By providing three distinct families — one optimized for interface clarity, one for geometric display, and one for traditional literary reading — plus Nastaliq for Urdu and Persian — we honor that complexity rather than flattening it into a single generic fallback. Similarly, Old North Arabian and Old South Arabian represent writing systems that flourished across the Arabian Peninsula centuries before Arabic script emerged, reminding us that the region's written heritage is layered, diverse, and far older than any single tradition.

Even Cuneiform — the oldest writing system of all — is rendered with the same care given to modern interfaces. Anatolian Hieroglyphs connect us to the Hittite Empire. Ahom preserves a living revival movement in Assam. Adlam, created within a single generation, demonstrates that new scripts continue to be born to meet community needs. Respect extends to your device as well: no unnecessary libraries are downloaded, no heavy animation frameworks are loaded, no scripts run in the background doing work you never asked for. Every kilobyte sent serves a purpose, and every interaction is predictable. Whether you visit from a modern desktop on fiber optics or an older mobile device on a shared connection, the content arrives quickly and remains fully functional. That is not an accident — it is a design decision made at every layer of this project.

Learn About These Scripts

What Are These Scripts?

Each writing system included here represents a distinct cultural and historical tradition — spanning from the dawn of recorded history in Mesopotamia through the monumental inscriptions of ancient Arabia and the imperial records of Anatolia, to the calligraphic traditions of Persia and the newly invented scripts of modern Africa. Below is an extended overview of their origins, characteristics, evolution, and current status — with representative sample text for every script presented in its proper form, directionality, and visual style.

Cuneiform

Cuneiform is the earliest known writing system in human history, emerging in southern Mesopotamia — modern-day Iraq — around 3400 BCE. Its name derives from the Latin cuneus, meaning "wedge," after the characteristic wedge-shaped marks impressed into wet clay tablets with a reed stylus. Originating as a system of pictographic symbols used for administrative record-keeping in the city of Uruk, it evolved over millennia into a versatile mixed script combining logograms, syllabic signs, and determinatives — markers of category or meaning — capable of recording not just accounts but literature, law, astronomy, diplomacy, and epic poetry.

For more than 3,000 years, cuneiform served as the primary writing system across the Near East, adapted to write Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian, Assyrian, Hittite, and other languages. Thousands of tablets have survived, preserved by fire and time, forming the oldest continuous written record of human civilization. The script fell out of use during the first century CE, and its meaning remained lost until the 19th century, when scholars deciphered it through painstaking comparison of multilingual inscriptions. Today, new editions and translations continue to expand our understanding of the ancient world, revealing details of daily life, religion, science, and politics that would otherwise have vanished entirely from human memory.

Old North Arabian

Old North Arabian refers to a collection of closely related ancient writing systems used across the Arabian Peninsula from roughly the 8th century BCE through the 4th century CE. Unlike the Arabic script that would emerge later, these were strictly alphabetic and did not connect letters; each character stood independently, typically inscribed in stone from right to left. Several distinct variants have been identified — including Safaitic, Hismaic, Thamudic, and Dadanitic — each named after the regions or tribes where they were most commonly found. Thousands of inscriptions survive, ranging from brief graffiti marking territory or recording lineage to longer invocations, prayers, and historical notes left by travelers and nomadic communities across what is now Saudi Arabia, Jordan, Syria, and northern Arabia.

These scripts are not direct ancestors of the Arabic script, though they share a distant common origin in earlier Semitic writing traditions. They represent a separate, parallel development that flourished alongside other writing systems of the ancient Near East before gradually fading as Arabic became the dominant written language across the peninsula. Decipherment began in the 19th century, and most letters are now well-understood, though some dialectal variations and archaic expressions continue to challenge scholars. What the inscriptions reveal is a landscape of remarkable linguistic continuity — communities speaking closely related Arabic dialects long before the standardization of the literary language and the spread of Islam. They offer a rare window into pre-Islamic beliefs, social structures, and naming practices that would otherwise be largely undocumented.

Old South Arabian — Musnad

Old South Arabian, best known by its traditional name Musnad, was the monumental writing system of ancient Yemen and the surrounding region, used continuously for more than 1,500 years — from approximately 1200 BCE until the 6th century CE. It served the great kingdoms of Saba', Himyar, Ma'in, Qataban, and Hadhramaut — wealthy trading civilizations that controlled the ancient incense routes linking Arabia to the Mediterranean and East Asia. The script was highly standardized, geometric, and symmetrical: letters were carefully spaced, never connected, and inscribed with remarkable precision on stone monuments, temple walls, and stelae. Most inscriptions are formal dedications, royal decrees, or building records, composed in an elevated literary language distinct from the everyday tongues of the people.

Musnad was written in either direction — right-to-left or left-to-right — and occasionally alternated line by line in a pattern known as boustrophedon. Its 29 distinct letters correspond closely to the phonemes of the ancient South Arabian languages, which survive today in a handful of isolated Modern South Arabian tongues spoken by small communities in Yemen and Oman. As Islam spread across the peninsula in the 7th century, the Arabic script replaced Musnad, and the ancient writing system fell out of common use. It was not forgotten entirely, however: interest in its revival has grown alongside cultural heritage work in Yemen and Oman, and modern digital fonts now ensure its preservation and accessibility for future generations.

Arabic

Arabic is one of the world's most widely used writing systems, serving as the primary script for more than 420 million people across the Middle East, North Africa, and beyond. Its origins trace to pre-Islamic times in the Arabian Peninsula, evolving from earlier North Arabian traditions by the 4th century CE. By the 7th century and the rise of Islam, the script had crystallized into a standardized literary vehicle that would spread across three continents. Written from right to left, it is inherently cursive: letters connect to their neighbors and change form depending on position — initial, medial, final, or isolated — creating a fluid, continuous visual rhythm that distinguishes it from most other writing systems.

This structural flexibility has given rise to an extraordinary diversity of calligraphic traditions — Kufic, Naskh, Thuluth, Ruq'ah, Diwani, and many others — each with its own rules, aesthetic, and historical purpose. Over centuries, the script has been adapted to write dozens of languages beyond Arabic itself: Persian, Urdu, Kurdish, Pashto, Sindhi, Malay, and others, each adding or modifying letters to represent sounds not found in Arabic. Today, three principal digital families serve distinct needs: a modern sans for interfaces and daily reading, Kufi for bold geometric display, and Naskh for traditional literary text — all preserving the cursive connectivity that defines the script.

Nastaliq — Urdu, Persian, and Beyond

Nastaliq is the most celebrated and visually distinctive of the Persian calligraphic traditions. Its name derives from a contraction of naskh ta'liq, meaning "suspended Naskh," describing its characteristic sloping, hanging form — words cascade diagonally downward from right to left across the page, creating a rhythm unmatched by any other cursive system. Developed and refined in Persia between the 13th and 16th centuries, it became the preferred script for Persian poetry and court documents, and as the Mughal Empire rose in India, it traveled east to become the standard writing system for Urdu. Today it remains the dominant calligraphic style for Urdu and Shahmukhi Punjabi, and is still widely used for literary and religious publications in Persian.

Unlike Arabic cursive styles where letters rest along a relatively horizontal baseline, Nastaliq requires every word to be positioned and angled with precision relative to those around it. It is essentially a calligraphic art form as much as a writing system — historically mastered only after years of disciplined practice. Digital implementation is equally complex, requiring advanced font shaping technology to replicate the fluid diagonal alignment automatically. What appears on screen is not simply a static typeface but a carefully engineered system of proportional relationships, kerning adjustments, and alternate letter forms working in concert to preserve the script's living, organic flow.

Anatolian Hieroglyphs — Luwian

Anatolian Hieroglyphs, also known as Luwian Hieroglyphs, served as the primary writing system of the Hittite Empire and its successor states in what is now Turkey from approximately 1400 BCE to 700 BCE. The script is mixed: it combines ideographic symbols representing whole words or concepts with phonetic signs spelling out syllables, creating a visual language that was both monumental and surprisingly versatile. Most surviving inscriptions appear on stone — royal monuments, rock faces, and stelae — composed to proclaim lineage, record military victories, or mark land boundaries. The signs are bold, pictorial, and distinct, often resembling the objects they originally depicted.

For centuries, the script was entirely undeciphered, its meaning lost to time. Progress began in the early 20th century, and by the 1980s a working understanding had emerged, though some signs and readings remain subjects of scholarly debate. What the inscriptions reveal is the continuity of Luwian culture long after the fall of the Hittite Empire — a patchwork of independent kingdoms preserving ancient traditions in the face of new regional powers. The script stands as a reminder that writing does not always spread through diffusion from a single center; it can emerge, flourish, and endure entirely within one region, serving communities whose names and stories might otherwise have vanished from history.

Ahom

The Ahom script was the writing system of the Ahom Kingdom, which ruled over much of Upper Assam in northeastern India for nearly six centuries — from the early 13th century until British annexation in 1826. Brought to the region by the Ahom people, who migrated from what is now Yunnan in southern China, the script was used to record royal chronicles, religious texts, genealogies, and practical knowledge — medicine, astrology, and rituals — on strips of dried palm leaf. The letters are rounded and flowing, shaped by the writing tool and material: an iron stylus used to incise characters into the leaf surface, which was then rubbed with ink to make the marks visible.

Following the end of Ahom rule, the script fell into decline, largely replaced by the Eastern Nagari (Assamese) script. It was not forgotten, however, and in recent decades a growing cultural revival has reclaimed it. Educators, activists, and community leaders are working to teach the script to younger generations, publish books, and ensure its digital survival. The Ahom script is no longer merely a relic of the past; it has become a living symbol of cultural continuity and resilience. Every digitized text, every font, and every page written in Ahom today is an act of restoration.

Adlam

Adlam is the youngest script represented here — and one of the most inspiring examples of community-led writing system creation in modern history. It was invented in the late 1980s by two teenage brothers, Ibrahima and Abdoulaye Barry, living in Guinea. Their goal was to create a writing system specifically for the Fulani language — spoken by millions across West and Central Africa but historically written using scripts adapted from other languages, each with its own limitations and cultural associations. The name itself is an acronym in Fulani: Alkule Dandayɗe Leñol Mulngo, meaning "the script that was created so that the people can preserve their culture."

Adlam is written from right to left and includes two distinct forms: a connected cursive style used in publications and daily writing, and an unjoined form taught to beginners. It was designed from the beginning to be intuitive, phonetically consistent, and culturally distinct — a visual symbol of Fulani identity. From a handful of early handwritten notebooks, the script has spread across borders, adopted by communities in Guinea, Nigeria, Senegal, Mali, and beyond. It was formally encoded in Unicode in 2019, accelerating its adoption in digital spaces. What began as a family project is now a living, evolving writing system carrying a language and culture forward into the digital age.

Inclusion as Design

These scripts are not curiosities. They represent living heritage, revived identity, and continuous cultural expression — from the clay tablets of Sumer to the stone inscriptions of Yemen, from the hanging calligraphy of Persia to the classrooms of Guinea. Including them alongside Latin, Cyrillic, and Greek is a deliberate choice: digital spaces belong to all the world's writing traditions, not only the most widely encountered. When typefaces are available and properly implemented, every script can be clear, legible, and dignified on screen. Respect in design means seeing — and serving — everyone.

Too often, web development proceeds as if "internationalization" means supporting a handful of major languages and treating everything else as optional. The result is a digital world that appears monolingual despite humanity's actual diversity. Changing this requires more than adding font links. It means designing with these scripts in mind from the beginning: allocating space for right-to-left layout, testing line-breaking behavior, adjusting vertical spacing where scripts require it, and accepting that what looks "right" for Latin text may be wrong — or even unreadable — for another system.

Nastaliq demonstrates this vividly: standard Arabic line spacing compresses it into illegibility; standard word-spacing rules disrupt its diagonal rhythm; Latin-centric layout assumptions can break its entire visual structure. Even Cuneiform requires generous letter spacing and appropriate sizing to distinguish individual signs clearly — it was never meant to be crammed tightly like Latin text. Old South Arabian, with its symmetrical monumental proportions, demands different spacing than cursive Arabic. Adlam's two forms serve different educational and publishing purposes and both deserve proper representation. Honoring each script means adjusting the design to fit the script — never expecting the script to adapt itself to the design.

From the oldest known writing to the youngest, from stone to screen, from lost to revived — every script here matters. Every one tells a story no other can. Every one deserves to be seen clearly, read respectfully, and preserved digitally. Good design is universal by choice, not by assumption.

About This Project

This project began as an exploration: what would a clean, sustainable, respectful website look like if it were built entirely without the clutter, tracking, and heavy frameworks that define so much of the modern web? The answer is Clarity — a working demonstration, a template, and a living document of thoughtful web practice. It is offered not as the only way to build a site, but as one example of how it might be done differently: with restraint, transparency, and care for the visitor.

Origins and Motivation

The impetus grew from observing how the internet has evolved. Heavy application frameworks load megabytes of code to display paragraphs and images that could be served in kilobytes. Dependencies create chains of maintenance that break unpredictably. Tracking systems follow visitors across pages and sites, often without clear notice or meaningful consent. Advertising interrupts reading, slows connections, and clutters interfaces. These burdens are not shared equally: they fall heaviest on those with limited bandwidth, older devices, restricted data plans, or assistive technology needs. A return to fundamentals — semantic HTML, modest CSS, and unobtrusive scripting — is not a step backward; it is a deliberate choice toward efficiency, longevity, and genuine inclusion.

Every decision here flows from one simple premise: the fundamental purpose of a website is to communicate. All other elements — styling, navigation, animation, behavior — exist to serve that purpose, never to obscure it. Content leads; presentation supports; functionality enhances. When someone visits, they should find what they came for without performance penalty, visual distraction, or privacy cost. That standard applies whether they are on a flagship smartphone over fiber or a basic feature phone on a shared connection; whether they have perfect vision or use a screen reader; whether they are fluent in the dominant language or exploring scripts they have never seen before.

Typography as Inclusion

The range of writing systems included is not merely decorative. It is a statement: digital spaces belong to everyone. Cuneiform connects us to the dawn of recorded human experience. Old North Arabian and Old South Arabian reveal the layered, pre-Islamic written heritage of the Arabian Peninsula. Arabic — in its three principal forms — represents one of the world's great literary and calligraphic traditions. Nastaliq carries the poetry of Urdu and Persian. Anatolian Hieroglyphs preserve the voices of ancient Anatolia. Ahom stands as a symbol of cultural renewal in Assam. Adlam demonstrates that new scripts continue to be born when communities need them. Latin, Cyrillic, and Greek remain essential global standards, presented here with the same clarity and respect given to every other system.

Treating all scripts with equal care means acknowledging that "default" is not universal. Directionality changes. Cursive connectivity alters spacing. Line height requirements differ. Sign density and visual weight vary. Building with this in mind requires more than linking fonts; it means designing from the beginning with multiple writing systems in view, testing each one, and accepting that the most familiar conventions are not the only correct ones.

Guiding Principles

Three core principles guide every choice in this project:

Legibility First. Text is the primary medium. Typefaces are selected for readability; contrast meets accessibility standards; spacing guides the eye without distraction. Animation serves function, not ornament. Nothing draws attention away from what is being communicated.

Transparency. The code is as readable as the content. Structure is semantic; styling is organized; behavior is minimal. Nothing is hidden. Anyone who wishes to understand how the site works can examine it, learn from it, and adapt it to their own needs.

Respect. Attention, device resources, and privacy are valued. No unnecessary resources load. No data is collected. No time is wasted. Every byte served has a purpose; every interaction is predictable; every visitor is treated equally.

Designers exploring multilingual typography will find real examples of right-to-left layout, cursive shaping, and historically significant writing systems working alongside modern interfaces. Typography and Typefaces

Typography is not decoration — it is the architecture of reading. Every choice here — from font selection to line height, spacing, and color — directly affects how easily, comfortably, and respectfully information is received. For a site presenting writing systems from across the world and throughout history, typography is not merely a design concern; it is the core subject matter itself. Getting it right matters.

The Noto Philosophy

All typefaces used belong to the Noto family, commissioned by Google and developed collaboratively with foundries, linguists, and community advisors globally. The name derives from the Latin nota, meaning "written mark or character," and the project's stated mission is to ensure that every language in the world can be represented equally on any device. Before Noto, different scripts were often rendered using unrelated fonts with inconsistent proportions, visual weights, and styles — pages appeared disjointed, and non-mainstream scripts were often reduced to generic fallbacks. Noto was built to address this by providing a unified visual foundation across writing systems.

Released under the SIL Open Font License — a permissive license specifically designed for typefaces — the fonts are free to use, modify, and redistribute. They are available in Sans, Serif, and Mono variants; multiple weights and styles; and individually for specific scripts so that visitors only download what their device needs. This combination of comprehensiveness, consistency, and open licensing makes Noto uniquely suited to a project like Clarity.

Typeface Selection

Each script has been assigned the specific Noto variant best suited to its visual tradition and function:

Noto Sans / Display / Mono — Global Latin, Cyrillic, Greek. Sans provides body text clarity; Display refined heading proportions; Mono ensures consistent character width for code and technical notation.

Noto Serif Ahom — Matches the traditional stroke contrast and flow of palm-leaf manuscript writing, preserving the script's historical appearance.

Noto Sans Arabic — Primary interface and body text font; modern, open, highly legible at all sizes.

Noto Kufi Arabic — Geometric, bold, monumental; used for headings and display where visual impact is appropriate.

Noto Naskh Arabic — Traditional calligraphic form; closer to long-established literary publishing conventions.

Noto Nastaliq Urdu — Specialized implementation of the sloping, hanging Nastaliq style; requires advanced font shaping technology to render correctly.

Noto Sans Adlam / Adlam Unjoined — Two distinct variants serving different purposes: joined cursive for publications and daily use; unjoined separated forms specifically designed to support early learners.

Noto Sans Anatolian Hieroglyphs — Preserves the monumental, pictorial character of Luwian royal inscriptions.

Noto Sans Cuneiform — Clearly distinguishes the dense, wedge-shaped sign inventory of ancient Mesopotamian writing.

Noto Sans Old North Arabian — Angular, spaced, right-to-left; matches the appearance of pre-Islamic inscriptional traditions.

Noto Sans Old South Arabian — Symmetrical, monumental, highly consistent proportions; faithful to Musnad carving style.

Directionality and Layout

Directionality is not a minor detail — it changes the entire visual experience of reading. Arabic, Nastaliq, and Adlam are written right-to-left; Latin, Cyrillic, Greek, and Cuneiform are written left-to-right. Old North Arabian and Old South Arabian may appear in either direction depending on historical period and inscription type. This site respects each script's natural flow: text alignment, spacing, and interaction patterns follow the writing system, not a single global default.

Where both directions appear on the same page — such as explanatory text alongside samples — the browser's bidirectional algorithm handles transitions automatically when properly structured. This is why semantic HTML matters: correct tagging enables correct rendering without manual adjustment for every phrase.

Spacing and Line Height

Scripts require different vertical spacing. Nastaliq, with its descending diagonal flow, needs generous line height to prevent overlapping. Cursive Arabic and Adlam benefit from slightly increased leading compared to Latin text. Monumental scripts such as Musnad and Anatolian Hieroglyphs are best presented with ample surrounding space. Latin text, by contrast, reads efficiently at tighter intervals. Rather than applying a single line height to all content, spacing is adjusted per script family so that each is comfortable to read on its own terms.

Performance and Loading

All fonts are requested from Google Fonts using a single combined request that specifies exactly what is needed. Preconnect hints establish early connections to font delivery domains, reducing perceived load time. The font-display: swap directive ensures readable text appears immediately while custom typefaces load in the background — no blank space, no blocked content, no delay. On repeat visits, fonts are cached by the browser and load instantly. These details matter especially on slower connections where every resource counts.

Material Symbols

Interface icons — theme toggle — are provided by Material Symbols, also open-source. They are included as a font rather than individual images, ensuring crisp rendering at any display resolution, consistent alignment with surrounding text, and no additional network requests beyond the initial load. Under the Apache License 2.0, they are freely redistributable.

Design System

Every visual element on this site follows a consistent, documented system. Nothing is arbitrary; every color, spacing unit, and transition is defined once and reused throughout. This approach simplifies maintenance, ensures visual coherence, and makes it easy to adapt the design elsewhere.

Color Palette

The palette is restrained and functional. There are no decorative gradients, no distracting patterns, and no unnecessary color shifts. Three modes are supported: light, dark, and auto. Auto follows system preference; manual selection persists in local storage so the site remembers your choice across visits.

In light mode, backgrounds are neutral white through soft off-white, with dark gray text for optimal readability without harsh pure black. The accent color is a clear medium blue used sparingly — for links, interactive elements, and emphasis — never overwhelming. Borders and dividers are subtle intermediate grays that provide structure without drawing attention.

In dark mode, backgrounds shift to deep neutral tones, text becomes light, and the accent brightens slightly for visibility. Contrast ratios meet WCAG accessibility standards throughout — text remains legible regardless of mode or device brightness.

All transitions between modes are smooth and brief — roughly 180 milliseconds — long enough to be perceived as intentional, short enough not to feel sluggish. Content fades in gently when pages change, maintaining continuity without distraction.

Spacing and Rhythm

Vertical spacing follows a consistent scale: paragraphs, headings, and sections are separated in predictable increments so the page has clear visual rhythm. Margins, padding, and line lengths are chosen to keep text comfortably readable — lines are neither too short (excessive eye movement) nor too long (difficult to track back to the next line). On narrow screens, layout reflows gracefully; navigation adapts; font sizes scale; content remains centered and accessible.

Navigation

Navigation is always visible at the top. The brand name links home; page links show which section is currently active; the theme toggle is immediately accessible. Pages are switched instantly without browser reload — content is already present, simply revealed. This eliminates loading spinners, reduces server requests, and creates a smooth, responsive experience. Scroll position resets to the top when navigating so every page starts fresh.

Responsive Behavior

The layout adapts continuously. No fixed breakpoints arbitrarily restyle content; instead, proportions adjust naturally as viewport dimensions change. Navigation wraps horizontally on small screens; sample blocks reflow; font sizes scale appropriately. Every writing system remains legible at every width — Nastaliq, Arabic, and Adlam maintain correct directionality and spacing regardless of device.

Code Philosophy

HTML is semantic: elements describe their purpose, not their appearance. CSS uses custom properties so design values are defined in one place and applied consistently. JavaScript is minimal and unobtrusive — it enhances behavior but does not create content. If scripting is disabled, all pages remain fully readable and navigable via standard links. The site functions without JavaScript as a fully functional static document.

Full persistent navigation bar with active-page highlighting. Theme toggle accessible directly. Pages load instantly without browser refresh. Scroll resets to top on navigation. Responsive and Accessible

File Structure and Architecture

Single-Source Structure

All pages exist within one index.html file. JavaScript displays only the active page; no separate files are fetched. This simplifies deployment, ensures consistency across views, and reduces load latency.

Component Responsibilities

  • index.html — Content, structure, font links, page sections
  • style.css — Variables, themes, layout, typography, responsive rules
  • script.js — Page switching, theme persistence, initialization
  • LICENSE — Full MIT License terms
  • README.md — Deployment, usage, credits

Standards Compliance

HTML5 semantic elements; CSS3 custom properties and transitions; ES6+ JavaScript. Works in all modern browsers; degrades gracefully where features are unsupported. No build tools or compilation required.

Deployment Guide

GitHub Pages — Recommended Method

This is the recommended deployment method for this project, aligning with its version-control nature and open-source orientation. Begin by creating a new repository on GitHub, named either after your chosen project or simply username.github.io if you intend this to be your personal site. Upload all files — index.html, style.css, script.js, LICENSE, and README.md — to the repository. They should appear at the root level, not inside any subfolder.

Navigate to your repository's Settings tab, then select Pages from the sidebar. Under Source, choose main branch and leave the folder selection as / (root). Click Save. GitHub will begin building your site. Initial publication typically takes between one and three minutes. Once deployed, your site will be available at https://username.github.io/repo-name/ or, if using your personal repository, directly at https://username.github.io/.

Verification and Maintenance

Visit your live site and confirm that all pages load, fonts display correctly, and the theme toggle functions as expected. If fonts appear to be missing or unstyled, allow a moment for the Google Fonts requests to complete — first load may take slightly longer as styles and typefaces are cached. Subsequent visits will load significantly faster. When you update content, simply push changed files to your repository; GitHub Pages will rebuild automatically within minutes.

Alternative Hosting

Because this project consists entirely of static files, it will run on nearly any web hosting platform. Upload the same five files to your chosen provider via SFTP, Git, or their file manager, ensuring index.html remains at the document root. No server-side processing, database, or special configuration is required. The site will also run directly from a local folder in your browser without deployment.
